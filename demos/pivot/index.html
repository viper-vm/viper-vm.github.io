      How Pivot works

      A motion planner for your front door.

      Furniture sites tell you to compare a few numbers — width, depth, “diagonal depth”. That treats your sofa as a box and your home as one doorway. Real moves fail at the combination: a door that opens straight onto a narrow hallway, a landing with a low ceiling, a lift that's a few centimetres short.

      1. You describe the item — its real shape, not just a box: a sofa's seat, back and arms, a piano's keyboard, a table's legs.
      2. You walk the route — every door, hallway, turn, stair flight and lift, in order, with a tape measure.
      3. Pivot rehearses the move in 3D. Two searches grow at once — one from the van, one backwards from the room — trying tens of thousands of positions, tilts and pivots (it's the classic “piano mover's problem” from robotics). When they meet, the item fits.
      4. You get the playbook — the exact sequence (“stand it on its arm, back first, swing it left round the frame”), the tightest spot to the centimetre, and a sheet to hand to the movers.

      If Pivot can't find a way it says so honestly, shows how far the item got, and tests fixes for you — legs off, the door off its hinges, the largest size that would make it. A search that fails isn't a mathematical proof, so treat a very close “no” as “ask a professional”.
